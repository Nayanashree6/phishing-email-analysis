# Phishing Email Analysis

## 1. Introduction

Phishing is a type of cyberattack in which attackers use fraudulent emails, messages, or websites to trick people into revealing sensitive information, downloading harmful files, or sending money.

The purpose of this project is to analyse five sample emails, identify suspicious characteristics, distinguish phishing attempts from legitimate messages, and develop a safe reporting process and staff awareness guide.

**Note:** These are fictional educational examples. Real emails must be assessed using all available evidence, including the sender address, link destination, context, and email authentication information.

## 2. Analysis of Five Sample Emails

### Email 1: Suspicious Account Verification

**Sample email**

From: `security@example.invalid`

Subject: URGENT! Your account will be blocked today.

Message:

"Dear User,

Your account will be permanently blocked in 30 minutes. Click the link below to verify your password immediately.

Verify your account: [Suspicious verification link]

Failure to respond will result in permanent account suspension.

Security Department"

**Classification:** Phishing

**Warning signs:**

* Creates urgency and fear.
* Uses a generic greeting.
* Requests immediate account verification.
* Encourages the recipient to follow an unverified link.
* Uses a fictional example domain rather than a verifiable organizational sender.

**Safe action:** Do not click the link. Visit the organization's official website independently or contact its IT team.

### Email 2: Fake Prize Notification

**Sample email**

From: `winner@example.invalid`

Subject: Congratulations! You have won a prize.

Message:

"Congratulations!

You have been randomly selected to receive a valuable prize, even though you did not enter a competition.

Pay a small processing fee and provide your bank details to claim your reward.

Claim your prize now!"

**Classification:** Phishing

**Warning signs:**

* Unexpected prize notification.
* Requests payment to receive a reward.
* Requests sensitive financial information.
* Does not identify a verifiable competition.
* Uses an unverified sender.

**Safe action:** Do not pay the fee or share financial details. Verify the offer through an independently located official source.

### Email 3: Fake Password Reset

**Sample email**

From: `helpdesk@example.invalid`

Subject: Immediate password reset required.

Message:

"Your company password has expired.

Open the attachment and follow the instructions to prevent your account from being disabled.

Complete the password reset immediately."

**Classification:** Suspicious; likely phishing in this fictional scenario.

**Warning signs:**

* Creates pressure to act quickly.
* Directs the recipient to an unexpected attachment.
* Does not provide a verifiable internal support reference.
* Could lead to a fake login page or a malicious file.

**Safe action:** Do not open the attachment. Contact the IT help desk through a known, trusted channel.

### Email 4: Routine Meeting Invitation

**Sample email**

From: `manager@company.example`

Subject: Weekly team meeting — Thursday

Message:

"Hello Team,

Our weekly team meeting is scheduled for Thursday at 10:00 AM in Conference Room 2.

Please review the meeting agenda in our usual company collaboration system before the meeting.

Thank you,
Team Manager"

**Classification:** Genuine-looking educational example; not enough evidence to verify authenticity in a real environment.

**Positive indicators:**

* Expected workplace context.
* No request for passwords or financial information.
* No unusual urgency.
* Provides ordinary meeting information.

**Checks to perform:**

* Confirm the sender's actual address.
* Verify that the meeting is expected.
* Check that any collaboration-system link points to the known company service.

**Safe action:** If the meeting is expected and the sender is verified, handle it normally. Do not assume an email is genuine merely because it looks professional.

### Email 5: Routine Project Update

**Sample email**

From: `projectlead@company.example`

Subject: Project status update

Message:

"Hello Team,

The project status meeting notes are available in our usual company document system.

Please review the action items before Friday's meeting.

Contact me through our normal internal communication channel if you have questions.

Regards,
Project Lead"

**Classification:** Genuine-looking educational example; authenticity cannot be established from the message text alone.

**Positive indicators:**

* Fits an ordinary workplace activity.
* Does not request credentials or payment.
* Refers to an established work process.
* Does not create unusual pressure.

**Checks to perform:**

* Verify the sender.
* Confirm the document location independently.
* Inspect any link destination before opening it.
* Confirm unexpected requests through another trusted channel.

**Safe action:** Proceed only when the sender and context have been reasonably verified.

**Important:** A real email cannot be classified as genuine based on its wording alone. Attackers can imitate normal workplace messages, and genuine emails can sometimes contain unusual requests.

## 4. Phishing Warning-Signs Checklist

Before acting on an email, check the following:

* [ ] Is the sender's actual email address unexpected or misspelled?
* [ ] Does the message create unusual urgency, fear, or pressure?
* [ ] Does it request passwords, verification codes, or financial details?
* [ ] Does a link lead somewhere unexpected?
* [ ] Does the message contain an unexpected attachment?
* [ ] Does it offer an unexpected prize, refund, or payment?
* [ ] Does it ask you to bypass normal company procedures?
* [ ] Does it request a payment or bank-account change?
* [ ] Does the request differ from the sender's usual communication style?
* [ ] Can you verify the request through a trusted, independent channel?

One warning sign does not prove that an email is malicious. Multiple warning signs or an unusual request should prompt further checks.

## 5. Safe Phishing Reporting Process

### Step 1: Stop

Do not click links, open unexpected attachments, reply, or provide sensitive information.

### Step 2: Report

Use your organization's approved phishing-reporting button or forward the message to the designated security team according to company policy.

Keep the original message and its email headers if possible, because these may help investigators.

### Step 3: Verify Independently

If the email claims to be from a manager, bank, supplier, or IT department, contact that organization or person using a known phone number or trusted communication channel.

Do not use contact details supplied only in the suspicious email.

### Step 4: If You Already Clicked

* Stop interacting with the suspicious page or file.
* Do not enter additional information.
* Report the incident to IT or the security team immediately.
* If you entered a password, use a trusted device and official website to change it promptly.
* Tell the security team if you disclosed a verification code, financial details, or downloaded a file.
* Follow the security team's instructions about device isolation, account protection, and further investigation.

Do not delete evidence or attempt to investigate a suspicious attachment yourself.

### Step 5: Help Protect Others

Follow the security team's instructions. Do not circulate suspicious emails to colleagues as warnings unless the approved reporting process requires it.

## 6. One-Page Staff Awareness Guide

### STOP, CHECK, REPORT

**STOP**

* Be cautious of unexpected emails.
* Do not act under pressure.
* Never disclose passwords or verification codes through an unsolicited email request.

**CHECK**

* Examine the actual sender address.
* Look for urgency, unusual requests, unexpected attachments, and suspicious links.
* Verify payments and changes to bank details independently.
* Remember that a familiar name or professional appearance does not guarantee authenticity.

**REPORT**

* Use your organization's approved phishing-reporting method.
* Preserve the original email.
* Contact IT or security if you are uncertain.
* Report immediately if you clicked a suspicious link, opened an attachment, or disclosed information.

### Three Golden Rules

1. Think before you click.
2. Verify unusual requests independently.
3. Report suspicious emails quickly.

**Remember:** Reporting a suspicious email is the right action, even when you are unsure whether it is malicious.

## 7. Conclusion

This project demonstrates that phishing emails commonly rely on urgency, impersonation, suspicious links, unexpected attachments, and requests for sensitive information.

Employees can reduce the risk of successful phishing attacks by checking messages carefully, verifying unusual requests independently, and reporting suspicious emails through approved security channels.

No single warning sign proves that an email is malicious. Safe decisions require considering the context, sender, links, attachments, and available verification evidence.
